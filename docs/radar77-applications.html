ADAS applications: what the radar is for

System

Everything on the other pages exists to serve a handful of driving functions. This page shows what those functions ask of the radar, and how that reaches down to the chip: why a front radar needs Tx power and phase noise, why a corner radar needs a wide field of view and 4 GHz of bandwidth, and why every one of them needs safety monitors.

A typical modern car has one front radar and four corner radars ("5R"); premium and L2+/L3 cars replace the front radar with a 4D imaging radar. Today one RFCMOS chip family often covers all classes; the antenna, the chirp profile and the software differ.

Regulations and ratings that drive adoption

  • EU General Safety Regulation (EU) 2019/2144: makes AEB, emergency lane keeping and other assistance functions mandatory for new vehicle types from July 2022 and for all new registrations from July 2024, with pedestrian and cyclist AEB in later phases.
  • USA, FMVSS No. 127 (NHTSA final rule, 2024): requires AEB, including pedestrian AEB, on new light vehicles, with compliance from September 2029.
  • NCAP ratings (Euro NCAP, C-NCAP, IIHS and others) score AEB car-to-car, AEB VRU, junction, blind-spot and lane-change cases; top scores in practice need a front radar plus corner radars together with a camera.
  • Rules and protocols change often; check the current editions. The radio rules themselves are on the regulations page.

Market trends

  • More radars per car: from one front radar to five or more, driven by NCAP corner and junction scenarios.
  • 4D imaging radar for L2+ and L3: elevation measurement and many more channels, by cascading chips or with high-channel single chips.
  • Central processing: "satellite" radar heads send raw or range-FFT data over Ethernet or CSI-2 to a central computer instead of a full object list (data interfaces).
  • Antenna in package and launch-on-package remove the RF printed-circuit launch and simplify the module (package page).
  • Interference grows with the number of radars on the road, so mitigation becomes a requirement (interference).
  • In-cabin radar at 60 GHz for child-presence detection and occupant monitoring uses the same FMCW principles in a different band.
Values are typical for 77 GHz automotive radar SoCs and meant for planning; check your process, product and customer requirements.
