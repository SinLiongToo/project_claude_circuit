IF chain and ADC circuits

IF · basebandADC

After the mixer, the beat signal lives at IF (roughly 0.1–20 MHz). The IF chain shapes it for the ADC: a high-pass filter removes leakage and very near reflections, a programmable gain amplifier sets the level, an anti-alias filter stops folding, and the ADC with its decimation filter delivers samples to the digital front end. The system view is in the signal chain; the RF side is on the RF front-end page.

High-pass filter: equalising the range

In FMCW the IF frequency is proportional to range, and echo power falls as 1/R⁴. So close targets and Tx→Rx leakage arrive at low IF with enormous power, and far targets at high IF are weak. A 2nd-order HPF rises 40 dB per decade below its corner, which cancels the 40 dB per decade fall of the echo and flattens the spectrum. The ADC then needs far less dynamic range.

Echo power vs range2nd-order HPF, 300 kHzLevel at the ADC
f_IF = S · 2R / c S = chirp slope (Hz/s) R at the corner: R_c = f_c · c / (2S) e.g. f_c = 300 kHz, S = 60 MHz/µs → R_c ≈ 0.75 m 2nd-order HPF: |H(f)|² = (f/f_c)⁴ / (1 + (f/f_c)⁴)
  • Circuit: an RC high-pass at the TIA output (1st order) plus a second pole in the PGA feedback, or an active-RC Sallen-Key stage.
  • Fast settle: a switch shorts the resistor for the first microsecond of each chirp, so the DC step at ramp start dies quickly.
  • Trim: R or C banks set the corner per mode and absorb process spread (±20 % sheet R).

PGA, anti-alias filter and ADC driver

The PGA (circuit on the RF page) is set by the AGC so the strongest expected echo sits 6–10 dB below ADC full scale. The anti-alias filter must attenuate anything near fs ± BW that would fold into the band. A continuous-time ΣΔ ADC has an inherent anti-alias response, so the AAF can be a simple 2nd-order stage; a SAR needs a sharp 4th–6th order filter and a driver that can charge its sampling capacitor in time.

The jitter formula is the sampling-clock limit for a sine at the band edge; in a CT-ΣΔ the feedback DAC edge jitter behaves similarly for NRZ DACs. Typical radar designs are thermal- or jitter-limited, not quantisation-limited, which is why the ADC clock comes from a clean PLL output.

Saturation detectors

A strong echo very close to the radar, Tx-to-Rx leakage, or another radar can drive a stage of the receive chain into clipping. Clipping spreads energy into harmonics and spurs over all range bins: weak targets are lost and ghost targets appear, and nothing in the FFT result says why. Saturation detectors flag it, so the AGC can lower the gain, the DSP can drop the affected chirp, and the safety monitor knows when the receiver is blind. Each stage clips at a different level, so each stage that can saturate first gets its own detector.

Testing the detectors

  • Threshold accuracy: the IF test DAC (BIST path C) steps a tone up through the threshold; the amplitude where the flag sets is the measured threshold, which is trimmed and checked against limits.
  • RF detector: the RF loopback with a low attenuator code gives a known high level at the LNA (level plan).
  • Latent faults: at key-on, each detector is forced (test-mode override or a large test tone) to prove the flag, the counter and the clear logic work (key-on tests).
  • Production: threshold and response time are tested at wafer sort; a detector that never fires is otherwise invisible in normal tests.

Decimation and the hand-over to the DFE

  • CIC filter first (no multipliers, decimates by 4–16), then half-band FIR stages (each ÷2), then a compensation FIR that flattens the passband droop.
  • Output: 16-bit complex samples (I + jQ) at 2× the IF bandwidth, per Rx channel, into the DFE and range FFT.
  • The DFE also removes residual DC, corrects I/Q gain and phase (coefficients from the loopback I/Q test), and flags ADC clipping for the AGC.
