Frequency synthesizer: PLL, VCO and chirp generation

LO · 20 → 77 GHz

The synthesizer is the heart of an FMCW radar: it makes the chirp. A fractional-N PLL locks a ~20 GHz VCO to a 40 MHz crystal and steps its division ratio every few nanoseconds so the frequency ramps linearly; a ×4 multiplier then brings the chirp to 76–81 GHz for the Tx and the Rx mixers. Its phase noise, ramp linearity and spurs set how close and how small a target the radar can still see (see the signal-chain overview).

Inside the blocks: charge pump, loop filter, LC tank, ×4

The loop diagram hides the circuits that set the synthesizer's performance. These are the four that matter most, each drawn at transistor or component level with the equations that tie it to the loop.

Charge pump + loop filter

Open-loop gain (type-II): G(s) = (I_CP / 2π) · Z_LF(s) · (2π·Kvco / s) / N Zero: ω_z = 1 / (R1·C1) Pole: ω_p2 ≈ 1 / (R1·C2) (C1 ≫ C2) Loop bandwidth (approx.): ω_c ≈ I_CP · R1 · 2π·Kvco / (2π·N) Design for ω_z ≈ ω_c / 4, ω_p2 ≈ 4·ω_c → phase margin ≈ 50–60°
  • UP/DN current mismatch and switch charge injection leave a net charge every reference cycle, even when locked, which appears as reference spurs; matched sources and dummy switches reduce it.
  • Leakage on the V_cp / Vtune node (switch Ioff, varactor gate) must be replaced every cycle, adding another spur source.
  • Kvco changes per band, so I_CP is often programmable to keep ω_c constant across bands.
  • Loop-filter resistors add thermal noise and the capacitors take large area; C1 is usually the biggest capacitor on the synthesizer.

VCO: cross-coupled LC tank

f0 = 1 / (2π·√(L · C_total)) C_total = C_fixed + C_var(Vtune) + Σ C_bank,k + C_par Tank Q: 1/Q = 1/Q_L + 1/Q_C (switch R_on lowers Q_C) Start-up: gm(M1,M2) · R_p ≥ 2–3, R_p = Q · ω0 · L Swing: V_tank ≈ (4/π) · I_tail · R_p (current-limited), capped by VDD and oxide stress
  • Inductor: a top-metal spiral or single turn, ≈ 100–300 pH at 20 GHz; its Q (≈ 15–25) usually dominates phase noise.
  • Varactors: accumulation-mode MOS for good Cmax/Cmin; a larger varactor gives more fine-tuning range but raises Kvco and noise sensitivity.
  • Capacitor bank: binary-weighted MOM capacitors with switch transistors; wide switches lower R_on (better Q) but add off-state capacitance (less range). This trade-off sets the number of bands.
  • Tail current: sets the swing; a tail filter (or no tail, with a resistor) reduces flicker-noise up-conversion into close-in phase noise.

Buffer + ×4 multiplier

  • Why ×4 instead of a 77 GHz VCO: a 20 GHz tank has much higher Q (lower phase noise), wider tuning range, and easier dividers; the price is +12 dB phase noise through the multiplier and the multiplier's power and area.
  • Buffer: isolates the tank from the multiplier load, so drive changes don't pull the VCO frequency.
  • Harmonic suppression: leftover f, 3f and 5f components become spurs at the Tx; typically kept below −30 dBc by the push-push symmetry plus tuned loads.
  • Drive level: each doubler needs enough input swing (see the swing table below); too little drive lowers the 77 GHz LO power to the PA and mixers.

Frequency plan

f_VCO = N.frac × f_ref f_RF = 4 × f_VCO f_ref = 40 MHz → N = 475 … 506.25 for f_VCO = 19.0 … 20.25 GHz (RF 76 … 81 GHz) ΣΔ step (24-bit fraction): f_ref / 2^24 ≈ 2.4 Hz VCO slope = RF slope / 4 e.g. 100 MHz/µs at 77 GHz → 25 MHz/µs at the VCO Phase noise: L_RF = L_VCO + 20·log10(4) = L_VCO + 12 dB

How it locks

  1. Power-up and reference: the crystal starts (ms) and the LDOs settle; the VCO starts oscillating once its core gain exceeds the tank loss (below).
  2. Coarse tuning, band calibration (AFC): with the loop open and Vtune held at mid-range, a counter compares the divided VCO with the reference and a binary search sets the capacitor-bank code. This brings the VCO within one band (typically tens of µs).
  3. Fine tuning, phase lock: the loop closes. The PFD compares REF and DIV edges and outputs UP or DN pulses as wide as the phase error; the charge pump turns them into current, the loop filter into Vtune, and the VCO moves until the edges align. The lock detector declares lock when the UP/DN pulse width stays inside a small window for many cycles (a few µs to tens of µs).
  4. Ramp tracking: during a chirp the loop must follow N changing every cycle. Tracking error grows with the slope and falls with loop bandwidth; fast chirps add two-point modulation (a feed-forward step injected at the VCO as well as the divider) or pre-distorted ramps.
  5. Reset between chirps: the ramp jumps back; the loop must re-settle during the idle time before the ADC samples the next chirp.

VCO design

  • Topology: an LC oscillator, usually a cross-coupled NMOS (or CMOS) pair driving a tank of an on-chip inductor, MOS varactors (fine tuning by Vtune) and a switched-capacitor bank (coarse bands). A tail current source or resistor sets the bias.
  • Start-up condition: the pair's negative resistance must cancel the tank loss with margin: gm · Rp ≥ 2–3, where Rp = Q·ω·L is the tank's parallel loss resistance. Check at the slow corner and hot, where gm is lowest and Q may drop.
  • Tuning range: the bands must cover 19.0–20.25 GHz at every process corner and temperature, so the design target is wider, typically about 18–21.5 GHz (≈ 17 %), with neighbouring bands overlapping by 10–20 %.
  • Kvco: sensitivity in MHz/V, typically a few hundred MHz/V per band. High Kvco makes the loop sensitive to Vtune noise and spurs; low Kvco needs more bands and more cap-bank switches (which lower Q). Kvco varies across bands, so the loop gain and bandwidth are compensated per band.
  • Phase noise (Leeson): L(Δf) ≈ 10·log[(2FkT/Ps)·(1 + (f0 / (2·Q·Δf))²)·(1 + fc/Δf)]. Higher tank Q and higher signal power lower it; flicker noise of the core (fc) adds close-in noise.
  • Amplitude: an amplitude control loop keeps the swing large enough for low phase noise and to drive the buffers, but below the level that stresses the varactors and gate oxides.

Why it won't lock: causes and debug flow

A PLL locks only if every link in the loop works: a reference, a running VCO that can reach the target frequency, a divider that counts correctly, a correction with the right sign and enough charge-pump headroom, a stable loop, and a lock detector that judges correctly. "Sometimes it doesn't lock" almost always means one of these links is marginal at a corner, a temperature or a supply level. Two on-chip readings find the broken link quickly: Vtune through the analog test mux, and the divided VCO frequency from the frequency counter.

  1. Reference: count it through the clock-observe mux.
  2. Open loop: hold Vtune at its minimum and maximum and count the VCO in every band. This gives the real tuning curves and shows whether the target is inside a band with margin.
  3. Close the loop and read Vtune: compare with the four patterns above.
  4. Counter on the output: a clean lock at the wrong frequency means the divider or its settings.
  5. Repeat at the edges: slow corner, hot and cold, minimum supply. Most intermittent failures only show there.
Why only sometimes? Band cal done cold and then the chip warms up (or the reverse); a VCO or prescaler that just meets its margin at one corner; supply noise during band cal making the counter pick the neighbouring band; band cal starting before the LDO or reference has settled; and Tx turning on and pulling the VCO. Choosing the band near mid-Vtune, waiting for supplies before band cal, and re-running band cal between frames remove most of them.
