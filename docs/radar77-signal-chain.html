00

Signal chain at a glance

The transmit and receive paths share one LO. A fractional-N PLL generates a linear frequency ramp at about 20 GHz. A ×4 multiplier brings it up to 77 GHz, and the result drives both the PA and the Rx mixers. Because each Rx mixer multiplies the echo by the chirp being transmitted at that moment, a target shows up as a low beat frequency (a few MHz). That is why a modest ADC can digitise a signal with GHz of bandwidth. Click a block to jump to its section.

Antenna, MIMO array and chip-to-antenna transition

RF · 77 GHz

At 77 GHz, λ₀ = 3.9 mm, so λ/2 is about 1.95 mm. That makes a 12- to 192-element virtual array fit in a palm-sized module. The antenna sets gain, beamwidth, sidelobes and, through the MIMO layout, angular resolution. For long-range designs it is usually the biggest factor in the link budget.

MIMO virtual array

With TDM, DDMA or BPM, each Tx/Rx pair acts as an element at position (x_tx + x_rx). Spacing the Tx elements N_rx·λ/2 apart fills a contiguous virtual array of N_tx·N_rx elements with λ/2 spacing. The aperture grows without adding Rx channels.

Design tip. The PCB patch array's centre frequency moves with εr and etch tolerance. Simulate over the ±tolerance corners in HFSS or CST, and use the on-chip Rx gain/phase calibration to absorb the channel mismatch that remains.

Tx multiplexing options

  • TDM: one Tx per chirp. Simple, with perfect separation, but v_max falls by a factor of N_tx and Doppler must be compensated before angle FFT.
  • BPM: all Tx on, with Hadamard phase codes across chirps. It gains SNR (a factor of N_tx in power) but needs the target to stay coherent over the code length.
  • DDMA: each Tx gets a slow-time phase ramp, so the Tx signals separate in the Doppler spectrum. Keeps full SNR, and is the choice for most imaging radars. Needs empty Doppler bands, or non-uniform offsets to resolve ambiguity.
Regulatory ceiling. ETSI EN 301 091 / EN 303 396 and FCC Part 95M limit peak EIRP to about 55 dBm, and also set mean-EIRP and out-of-band limits. With several Tx beamforming, P_t + G_ant + 20log N_tx can reach that limit, so check it with all Tx on.
04

Phase rotator and its control DACs

RF · 77 GHzDAC

Each Tx channel needs a fast, fine-grained phase control, used for BPM/DDMA MIMO codes, beam steering, and calibrating out channel mismatch. At 77 GHz the usual solution is an active vector-modulator phase rotator. It splits the LO into quadrature components I (0°) and Q (90°), weights each with a signed variable-gain stage set by a current DAC, and sums them:

v_out = a_I · LO_I + a_Q · LO_Q = A·cos θ · cos ωt + A·sin θ · sin ωt = A · cos(ωt − θ) a_I = round(M · cos θ_k) a_Q = round(M · sin θ_k) M = 2^(b−1) − 1 (sign-magnitude DAC) θ_k = k · 360° / 2^n n = phase bits (e.g. 6 → 5.625° step)

Phase shifter or phase rotator?

The two terms are often used for the same Tx block, but they aren't identical. Phase shifter is the general term for any circuit that applies a controlled phase shift. A phase rotator is a phase shifter that can turn the phase continuously through the full 360° and keep going past it with no jump (endless rotation). In practice that almost always means the I/Q vector-interpolation type shown below; SerDes and clock-recovery circuits use the same idea under the name phase interpolator. Datasheets use both names: TI's AWR parts, for example, call the block a "Tx phase shifter", but it is built as a vector-modulator rotator. This page calls it a phase rotator throughout.

The DAC implementation

  • Current-steering, sign-magnitude: binary-weighted LSBs plus unary (thermometer) MSBs for monotonicity. The sign bit switches the Gilbert cell's LO polarity, so the DAC itself is unipolar.
  • Output: the DAC current is the VGA tail current (or its gain-control bias). Keep the cascode headroom, and match the unit cells, placed in common-centroid layout.
  • Linearise in the LUT, not in analog: the VGA's gain vs. current is not linear. Measure the real (a_I, a_Q) → phase/amplitude map with the loopback BIST, and store the inverse as the LUT.
  • Timing: double-buffer the DAC registers and update them on the timing engine's chirp-start strobe, so every Tx switches in the same idle window.

DAC linearity specification

There's no radar-specific standard for DAC linearity. The terms and test methods follow IEEE Std 1658 (DAC terminology and test methods), and each block's limits come from its own error budget. For the rotator, the budget is simple geometry. An error of δ LSB on one axis at full-scale code M moves the output vector sideways by δ, which rotates it by up to δ/M radians when the error is perpendicular to the vector:

Δθ_max ≈ δ / M [rad] ΔA_max ≈ 20·log10(1 + δ/M) [dB] M = 2^(b−1) − 1 Target RMS phase error 2° with 7-bit DACs (M = 63): δ ≤ 63 · 2° · π/180 ≈ 2.2 LSB total per axis Split: quantisation (±0.5 LSB) + INL (≤ 1 LSB) + mismatch/drift margin → spec INL < 1 LSB, DNL < 0.5 LSB

What a DAC spec should define

  • Static: resolution, DNL, INL (state endpoint or best-fit), monotonicity, offset error, gain error, drift over −40…150 °C Tj
  • Dynamic (only for DACs that change during operation, such as the rotator and test tone): settling time to ½ LSB, glitch energy (pV·s), SFDR / THD at the relevant output frequency
  • Conditions: supply, temperature, and load (e.g. the VGA tail at its actual bias)

How it's verified on-chip

  • Rotator DACs: sweep all codes through the RF loopback; FFT phase and amplitude give INL/DNL directly (BIST recipe)
  • Test-tone DAC: its spectrum through the main ADC + FFT; separate DAC and ADC distortion by moving the tone or filtering it with the IF LPF
  • Trim / bias DACs: code sweep read back on the aux ADC to check monotonicity

05

Chirp synthesis: the upconversion path

LO · 20 → 77 GHz

FMCW radar has no separate upconversion mixer. The transmitted signal is the LO. A fractional-N PLL with a ramp generator (sigma-delta modulated divider) sweeps a VCO at about 19–20.25 GHz, and a frequency multiplier (×4, or ×2 from a ~38 GHz VCO) brings it up to 76–81 GHz. The same LO then drives every Rx mixer. This sharing is why close-in phase noise largely cancels at short range (range correlation).

Why the key specs matter

  • Ramp nonlinearity spreads a target's beat tone over several range bins, which blurs range and raises sidelobes around strong targets. Its effect grows with range, because the error builds up over the delay τ.
  • Phase noise is suppressed by the range-correlation term 2·sin(π·f_off·τ). It cancels for nearby clutter such as the bumper and radome, but at long range the phase-noise skirt of a strong reflector can hide a weak target nearby (a pedestrian next to a truck).
  • Spurs from the fractional-N divider show up as ghost targets. Keep them below about −70 dBc, or place them outside the IF band you use.
06

Receiver front end and I/Q downconversion

RF → IF

The echo, delayed by τ = 2R/c, mixes with the chirp being transmitted at that moment. Their difference is a constant beat frequency f_b = S·τ, plus a Doppler term f_d = 2v/λ. This "stretch processing" turns a 1–4 GHz sweep into a DC–20 MHz IF signal.

Real vs complex (I/Q) baseband

A real mixer folds positive and negative IF on top of each other, so noise from the image band adds about 3 dB. It also cannot tell interference or folded spurs apart from real targets. A complex I/Q baseband keeps the full F_s as usable IF bandwidth, rejects that noise, and makes it much easier to detect radar-to-radar interference. The cost is twice the IF and ADC hardware per channel.

07

I/Q generation, I/Q mixers and imbalance

RF · 77 GHzIF

A radar MMIC needs quadrature LO signals in three places: the Rx complex mixers (I and Q LO for each Rx), the Tx phase rotator (I and Q inputs to the vector modulator), and the loopback SSB modulator used by BIST. They all have the same problem: making two copies of a 76–81 GHz signal exactly 90° apart and equal in amplitude, over a 5 GHz band and −40…150 °C.

Generate quadrature at the final frequency

The LO is made at about 20 GHz and multiplied ×4, and the multiplier multiplies phase as well. A 90° I/Q split made at 20 GHz becomes 360° after ×4, which is useless. Quadrature therefore has to be created after the multiplier, at 77 GHz. The one exception is a deliberate 22.5° split at 20 GHz, which ×4 turns into 90°, but then every phase error is also multiplied by four.

Rx I/Q mixer design

  • Passive (switching) mixers in CMOS: best linearity and lowest flicker noise, which matters because near targets sit at low IF. They need a large, well-defined LO swing, so the LO buffers must be strong.
  • Active Gilbert mixers (common in SiGe): give conversion gain, need less LO drive, but have more flicker noise and lower linearity.
  • LO distribution to 4 Rx × (I, Q): use a symmetric H-tree or matched LO buffers so that the channel-to-channel phase error is design-matched, not just calibrated. LO leakage into the RF port creates DC offset (self-mixing); the HPF removes it.
  • After the mixer: I and Q each need their own IF chain and ADC, matched in gain and group delay, so baseband mismatch adds to the RF quadrature error.

What imbalance does, and how it's corrected

Model: I = cos(ωt) Q = g · sin(ωt + φ) (g: gain ratio, φ: quadrature error) IRR = (1 + g² + 2g·cos φ) / (1 + g² − 2g·cos φ) g = 0.5 dB, φ = 3° → IRR ≈ 28 dB g = 0.2 dB, φ = 1° → IRR ≈ 37 dB DFE correction: I' = I Q' = (Q/g − I·sin φ) / cos φ (g, φ from loopback SSB test)
  • Rx: the image of each target appears at −f_b. With an up-chirp and complex baseband, real targets sit on positive beat frequencies, so the image mostly falls in the half of the spectrum the processing ignores. The real costs are an image of strong clutter and interference in the negative half, which confuses interference detection and makes it harder to reuse that band. About 25–30 dB IRR analog, plus DFE correction to 40–50 dB, is usually enough.
  • Tx rotator: quadrature error there converts directly into phase and amplitude error per code. That is what the LUT in the phase rotator section cancels, so the Tx side is the more demanding user of I/Q accuracy.
  • Measurement: the loopback SSB tone at +f_m and its image at −f_m give g and φ in a single capture (see the BIST page).
08

IF conditioning: HPF, VGA, anti-alias filter

IF · DC–20 MHz

Echo power falls as R⁻⁴, and range maps linearly to beat frequency. A second-order high-pass filter (+40 dB/decade) therefore flattens the IF spectrum: it attenuates the strong close-in clutter and leakage, so distant echoes use more of the ADC's dynamic range. The VGA then sets the level, and the anti-alias filter bounds noise and aliasing.

ADC

Analog → Digital

Most radar MMICs use continuous-time sigma-delta (CT-ΣΔ) ADCs. Their input is resistive, the anti-aliasing is inherent, and they reach high dynamic range at a modest output rate. Time-interleaved or pipelined SAR ADCs appear where high IF bandwidth matters more (very fast chirps, short range).

10

Digital front end (DFE)

Digital
  • Decimation chain: the ΣΔ modulator runs at a few hundred MHz. CIC and half-band FIR stages bring it down to the programmed output rate and add the final channel filtering.
  • DC and I/Q correction: remove static DC offset and apply the gain/phase imbalance coefficients from calibration.
  • Interference detection: other radars cross your IF band as short, high-amplitude chirps. Detect them by amplitude or statistics in the time domain, then zero or interpolate the affected samples before the FFT. The alternative is a noise floor raised across the whole chirp.
  • Chirp quality flags: saturation, interference and monitor status are written into chirp headers.
  • Data path: packed samples go into the on-chip radar cube memory over DMA, or out through CSI-2 / LVDS for raw-data (satellite) architectures that process in a central ECU.
11

Radar processing: radar cube to object list

Digital
  1. Range FFT (per chirp, per Rx, windowed): runs on the fly in the hardware accelerator while the next chirp is being sampled.
  2. Doppler FFT (per range bin, across chirps): runs after the frame. Add TDM Doppler compensation or DDMA demultiplexing here.
  3. Non-coherent integration and CFAR on the range-Doppler map: CA-CFAR along range, OS-CFAR along Doppler, P_fa ≈ 10⁻⁶. Threshold SNR is about 12–15 dB.
  4. Angle estimation for each detected cell: FFT/DBF over the virtual array, or super-resolution (MUSIC, ESPRIT, IAA) for imaging radars. Apply the calibration matrix first.
  5. Velocity disambiguation by multi-profile Chinese-remainder methods or by tracking.
  6. Point cloud → clustering (DBSCAN) → tracking (EKF/UKF, IMM motion models) → classification. Also ego-motion estimation, and multipath and guardrail suppression.
  7. Output: objects and point cloud over CAN-FD (2–5 Mbit/s) or 100/1000BASE-T1 Ethernet.

12

Monitoring and built-in self-test

Safety · ASIL-B

A slow SAR auxiliary ADC (10–12 bit, behind an analog mux) reads the power detectors, temperature sensors and supply dividers. Loopback paths inject known signals through the real Rx chain and main ADCs. The RF control CPU runs these monitors in the idle time between frames. The total must fit in the FTTI from the system safety concept.

Deep dive: Radar BIST & Loopback covers stimulus generation, loopback routing, on-chip measurement, trim feedback and instrument-free production test. Digital DFT & stress covers scan, ATPG, memory/logic BIST, burn-in, HVST, PAT and AEC-Q100.

Analog and RF monitors

  • Tx output power and reflected power (ball break)
  • Tx phase via Tx→Rx loopback
  • Rx gain, phase and noise figure via test-tone injection
  • IF filter response (swept IF test tone)
  • PLL lock, chirp start frequency and slope (counter against the reference)
  • LO distribution power (cascade)
  • Supplies and multiple die-temperature sensors

Design must-haves

  • Monitor ADC reference independent of the monitored bandgap (avoids a common-cause fault)
  • Detector trim stored in eFuse/OTP, plus temperature compensation
  • Weak output couplers (−15…−20 dB) so detection costs little PA power
  • Loopbacks fully isolated when off (no ghost targets)
  • Startup latent-fault tests: logic BIST, memory BIST, ADC self-check
  • FMEDA mapping from each monitor to the SPFM/LFM coverage it provides

Power management: supply tree and LDOs

Supply

A radar MMIC is a mixed-signal chip with very different loads: a 20 GHz VCO where microvolts of supply noise become FM, PAs that switch hundreds of mA on and off each chirp in TDM, IF and ADC circuits that turn supply ripple directly into IF spurs, and bursty digital FFT loads. The standard answer is efficient bucks off-chip, quiet LDOs on-chip, one LDO per sensitive domain, so that no two noise-sensitive blocks share a regulator.

How supply noise turns into radar errors

VCO pushing: Δf_RF = 4 · K_push · v_n (×4 multiplier) Ripple spur: L_spur ≈ 20log( 4·K_push·V_rip / (2·f_rip) ) [dBc] (outside PLL bandwidth) K_push = 20 MHz/V, V_rip = 10 µV @ 2 MHz → 800 Hz deviation → ≈ −74 dBc sidebands PA supply AM/PM: Δφ_Tx = K_AMPM · ΔV_PA (e.g. 10°/V × 10 mV droop = 0.1°)
  • Inside the PLL loop bandwidth (≈ 1–3 MHz) the loop suppresses VCO pushing. Outside it, only the LDO's PSRR helps. Buck converters switch at about 2 MHz, right at that edge, so VCO LDO PSRR at 1–10 MHz is the critical spec.
  • TDM Tx switching steps the PA current every chirp. If the LDO hasn't settled when the ADC window opens, each Tx sees a slightly different phase and gain, which looks like a MIMO calibration error that changes with the chirp timing. Size the idle time and the LDO's loop bandwidth together.
  • IF/ADC rails: ripple at f_rip appears directly as a tone at f_rip in every chirp: a fixed ghost at the range R = f_rip·c/(2S). Keep buck frequencies out of the used IF band, or sync the buck to the chirp so its harmonics fall in known bins that processing can blank.
  • Spread-spectrum bucks trade a clean spur for a raised noise floor, which is often worse for radar than a synchronised, known spur.

LDO design choices

TopologyStrengthWeaknessBest for
PMOS pass + external capLow dropout, stable, simplePSRR falls off above the loop bandwidth (≈ 100 kHz–1 MHz); needs a µF-class pin capIO, bandgap/aux, digital
NMOS source follower (cap-less)Fast transient, good high-frequency PSRR (the gate is isolated from the supply)Needs gate drive above V_in (charge pump) or more headroomPA/RF with Tx load steps
Cascaded: pre-regulator + LDOPSRR of the two stages adds (> 80 dB)More dropout (needs 1.8 V in for a 1.2 V out)VCO
Feed-forward ripple cancellationHigh PSRR at MHz without a large capMatching-dependent; more complexVCO, IF/ADC
  • Low-noise reference: filter the bandgap into the LDO with a large on-chip RC (or a sample-and-hold). The LDO then amplifies its own error-amplifier noise, so size the input pair for low 1/f noise.
  • Headroom: 1.8 V → 1.2 V leaves 600 mV for cascoding and high PSRR. 1.0 V → 0.9 V leaves little, so RF LDOs often trade PSRR for dropout, or are bypassed with a clean external 1.0 V.
  • Trim and monitoring: a trim DAC sets V_out (eFuse); UV/OV comparators and the aux ADC check each output. The LDO also has current limit and soft-start.
  • Sequencing: power-on reset holds the chip until all rails are valid. Enable LDOs in a fixed order (reference → analog → RF) so that no block is biased from a floating rail.

Calibration, verification, compliance

These steps run in order, from the first silicon to the vehicle.

  1. Wafer and final test: ATE with a 77 GHz loopback or probe; trim the power detectors, temperature sensors and bandgaps; screen NF, Pout and phase noise.
  2. Module EOL: anechoic chamber with a corner reflector (or target simulator) at several angles. Measure Rx/Tx gain and phase mismatch and store the calibration matrix in flash. Measure the antenna pattern and verify EIRP.
  3. Runtime calibration: temperature-indexed LUTs for Tx power and phase, and monitor-driven recalibration.
  4. Mounting alignment: EOL alignment on the vehicle, plus online auto-alignment from stationary targets and ego-motion.
  5. Radar target simulator (RTS) HIL: scenario and interference tests, ghost and multipath checks.
  6. Compliance: ETSI EN 301 091 / EN 303 396 or FCC Part 95M (EIRP, OOB, spurious); EMC; ISO 26262 safety case; cybersecurity (ISO/SAE 21434) for the SW and interfaces.
The figures are typical engineering ranges for current RFCMOS and SiGe automotive radar and are meant for early trade-offs. Always design against your chosen MMIC's datasheet and the current versions of the regulations.
