00

Signal chain at a glance

The transmit and receive paths share one LO. A fractional-N PLL generates a linear frequency ramp at about 20 GHz. A ×4 multiplier brings it up to 77 GHz, and the result drives both the PA and the Rx mixers. Because each Rx mixer multiplies the echo by the chirp being transmitted at that moment, a target shows up as a low beat frequency (a few MHz). That is why a modest ADC can digitise a signal with GHz of bandwidth. Click a block to jump to its section.

02

Antenna, MIMO array and chip-to-antenna transition

RF · 77 GHz

At 77 GHz, λ₀ = 3.9 mm, so λ/2 is about 1.95 mm. That makes a 12- to 192-element virtual array fit in a palm-sized module. The antenna sets gain, beamwidth, sidelobes and, through the MIMO layout, angular resolution. For long-range designs it is usually the biggest factor in the link budget.

MIMO virtual array

With TDM, DDMA or BPM, each Tx/Rx pair acts as an element at position (x_tx + x_rx). Spacing the Tx elements N_rx·λ/2 apart fills a contiguous virtual array of N_tx·N_rx elements with λ/2 spacing. The aperture grows without adding Rx channels.

Design tip. The PCB patch array's centre frequency moves with εr and etch tolerance. Simulate over the ±tolerance corners in HFSS or CST, and use the on-chip Rx gain/phase calibration to absorb the channel mismatch that remains.

Transmitter: phase shifter, PA, power control

RF · 77 GHz

Each Tx channel takes the 77 GHz LO, applies a programmable phase (for BPM/DDMA MIMO coding and beam steering), amplifies it, and sends it to the antenna. The coupler and power detector at the output feed the monitoring ADC.

Tx multiplexing options

  • TDM: one Tx per chirp. Simple, with perfect separation, but v_max falls by a factor of N_tx and Doppler must be compensated before angle FFT.
  • BPM: all Tx on, with Hadamard phase codes across chirps. It gains SNR (a factor of N_tx in power) but needs the target to stay coherent over the code length.
  • DDMA: each Tx gets a slow-time phase ramp, so the Tx signals separate in the Doppler spectrum. Keeps full SNR, and is the choice for most imaging radars. Needs empty Doppler bands, or non-uniform offsets to resolve ambiguity.
Regulatory ceiling. ETSI EN 301 091 / EN 303 396 and FCC Part 95M limit peak EIRP to about 55 dBm, and also set mean-EIRP and out-of-band limits. With several Tx beamforming, P_t + G_ant + 20log N_tx can reach that limit, so check it with all Tx on.
04

Chirp synthesis: the upconversion path

LO · 20 → 77 GHz

FMCW radar has no separate upconversion mixer. The transmitted signal is the LO. A fractional-N PLL with a ramp generator (sigma-delta modulated divider) sweeps a VCO at about 19–20.25 GHz, and a frequency multiplier (×4, or ×2 from a ~38 GHz VCO) brings it up to 76–81 GHz. The same LO then drives every Rx mixer. This sharing is why close-in phase noise largely cancels at short range (range correlation).

Why the key specs matter

  • Ramp nonlinearity spreads a target's beat tone over several range bins, which blurs range and raises sidelobes around strong targets. Its effect grows with range, because the error builds up over the delay τ.
  • Phase noise is suppressed by the range-correlation term 2·sin(π·f_off·τ). It cancels for nearby clutter such as the bumper and radome, but at long range the phase-noise skirt of a strong reflector can hide a weak target nearby (a pedestrian next to a truck).
  • Spurs from the fractional-N divider show up as ghost targets. Keep them below about −70 dBc, or place them outside the IF band you use.
05

Receiver front end and I/Q downconversion

RF → IF

The echo, delayed by τ = 2R/c, mixes with the chirp being transmitted at that moment. Their difference is a constant beat frequency f_b = S·τ, plus a Doppler term f_d = 2v/λ. This "stretch processing" turns a 1–4 GHz sweep into a DC–20 MHz IF signal.

Real vs complex (I/Q) baseband

A real mixer folds positive and negative IF on top of each other, so noise from the image band adds about 3 dB. It also cannot tell interference or folded spurs apart from real targets. A complex I/Q baseband keeps the full F_s as usable IF bandwidth, rejects that noise, and makes it much easier to detect radar-to-radar interference. The cost is twice the IF and ADC hardware per channel.

06

IF conditioning: HPF, VGA, anti-alias filter

IF · DC–20 MHz

Echo power falls as R⁻⁴, and range maps linearly to beat frequency. A second-order high-pass filter (+40 dB/decade) therefore flattens the IF spectrum: it attenuates the strong close-in clutter and leakage, so distant echoes use more of the ADC's dynamic range. The VGA then sets the level, and the anti-alias filter bounds noise and aliasing.

07

ADC

Analog → Digital

Most radar MMICs use continuous-time sigma-delta (CT-ΣΔ) ADCs. Their input is resistive, the anti-aliasing is inherent, and they reach high dynamic range at a modest output rate. Time-interleaved or pipelined SAR ADCs appear where high IF bandwidth matters more (very fast chirps, short range).

08

Digital front end (DFE)

Digital
  • Decimation chain: the ΣΔ modulator runs at a few hundred MHz. CIC and half-band FIR stages bring it down to the programmed output rate and add the final channel filtering.
  • DC and I/Q correction: remove static DC offset and apply the gain/phase imbalance coefficients from calibration.
  • Interference detection: other radars cross your IF band as short, high-amplitude chirps. Detect them by amplitude or statistics in the time domain, then zero or interpolate the affected samples before the FFT. The alternative is a noise floor raised across the whole chirp.
  • Chirp quality flags: saturation, interference and monitor status are written into chirp headers.
  • Data path: packed samples go into the on-chip radar cube memory over DMA, or out through CSI-2 / LVDS for raw-data (satellite) architectures that process in a central ECU.
09

Radar processing: radar cube to object list

Digital
  1. Range FFT (per chirp, per Rx, windowed): runs on the fly in the hardware accelerator while the next chirp is being sampled.
  2. Doppler FFT (per range bin, across chirps): runs after the frame. Add TDM Doppler compensation or DDMA demultiplexing here.
  3. Non-coherent integration and CFAR on the range-Doppler map: CA-CFAR along range, OS-CFAR along Doppler, P_fa ≈ 10⁻⁶. Threshold SNR is about 12–15 dB.
  4. Angle estimation for each detected cell: FFT/DBF over the virtual array, or super-resolution (MUSIC, ESPRIT, IAA) for imaging radars. Apply the calibration matrix first.
  5. Velocity disambiguation by multi-profile Chinese-remainder methods or by tracking.
  6. Point cloud → clustering (DBSCAN) → tracking (EKF/UKF, IMM motion models) → classification. Also ego-motion estimation, and multipath and guardrail suppression.
  7. Output: objects and point cloud over CAN-FD (2–5 Mbit/s) or 100/1000BASE-T1 Ethernet.

10

Monitoring and built-in self-test

Safety · ASIL-B

A slow SAR auxiliary ADC (10–12 bit, behind an analog mux) reads the power detectors, temperature sensors and supply dividers. Loopback paths inject known signals through the real Rx chain and main ADCs. The RF control CPU runs these monitors in the idle time between frames. The total must fit in the FTTI from the system safety concept.

Deep dive: Radar BIST & Loopback covers stimulus generation, loopback routing, on-chip measurement, trim feedback and instrument-free production test.

Analog and RF monitors

  • Tx output power and reflected power (ball break)
  • Tx phase via Tx→Rx loopback
  • Rx gain, phase and noise figure via test-tone injection
  • IF filter response (swept IF test tone)
  • PLL lock, chirp start frequency and slope (counter against the reference)
  • LO distribution power (cascade)
  • Supplies and multiple die-temperature sensors

Design must-haves

  • Monitor ADC reference independent of the monitored bandgap (avoids a common-cause fault)
  • Detector trim stored in eFuse/OTP, plus temperature compensation
  • Weak output couplers (−15…−20 dB) so detection costs little PA power
  • Loopbacks fully isolated when off (no ghost targets)
  • Startup latent-fault tests: logic BIST, memory BIST, ADC self-check
  • FMEDA mapping from each monitor to the SPFM/LFM coverage it provides

13

Calibration, verification, compliance

These steps run in order, from the first silicon to the vehicle.

  1. Wafer and final test: ATE with a 77 GHz loopback or probe; trim the power detectors, temperature sensors and bandgaps; screen NF, Pout and phase noise.
  2. Module EOL: anechoic chamber with a corner reflector (or target simulator) at several angles. Measure Rx/Tx gain and phase mismatch and store the calibration matrix in flash. Measure the antenna pattern and verify EIRP.
  3. Runtime calibration: temperature-indexed LUTs for Tx power and phase, and monitor-driven recalibration.
  4. Mounting alignment: EOL alignment on the vehicle, plus online auto-alignment from stationary targets and ego-motion.
  5. Radar target simulator (RTS) HIL: scenario and interference tests, ghost and multipath checks.
  6. Compliance: ETSI EN 301 091 / EN 303 396 or FCC Part 95M (EIRP, OOB, spurious); EMC; ISO 26262 safety case; cybersecurity (ISO/SAE 21434) for the SW and interfaces.
The figures are typical engineering ranges for current RFCMOS and SiGe automotive radar and are meant for early trade-offs. Always design against your chosen MMIC's datasheet and the current versions of the regulations.
