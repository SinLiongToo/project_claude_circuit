Digital DFT, scan test and stress screening

A radar SoC is mostly digital by transistor count: DSP, FFT accelerator, lockstep MCUs and several MB of SRAM for the radar cube. This page covers how that logic is made testable (scan, ATPG, compression, at-speed test, memory and logic BIST, JTAG), how production test stresses parts to catch latent defects (voltage stress, VLV, burn-in, temperature, IDDQ, PAT), and how AEC-Q100 qualification and ISO 26262 in-field test fit in. The analog/RF self-test is on the BIST & loopback page.

Why digital DFT matters in a radar SoC

Digital

Without test structures, a digital core with millions of flip-flops can't be tested in reasonable time: functional patterns reach only a small part of the internal states. Design for Test (DFT) adds controllability (set any internal state) and observability (read any internal state). Automatic pattern generation can then target every modelled fault.

DriverWhat it means for DFT
Automotive quality target"Zero defect" culture: field returns well below 1 DPPM. This needs very high fault coverage on several fault models, not just stuck-at.
Large digital contentDSP + HWA + lockstep R5F + 2–8 MB SRAM (single chip), far more on imaging SoCs. Scan data volume and test time dominate unless compressed.
ISO 26262 (ASIL-B)Latent faults in safety mechanisms must be detected in the field, so logic and memory BIST run at key-on (and sometimes periodically).
Mixed-signal chipScan must not disturb the analog/RF blocks, and high toggle activity during shift must not collapse the on-chip supplies.
CostTest time × tester cost per second is a large share of unit cost. Compression, multi-site and parallel BIST cut it.

Scan design, ATPG and test compression

Digital

Every flip-flop is replaced by a scan flip-flop: a D flop with a 2:1 mux in front. When SE (scan enable) = 1 it takes SI from the previous flop, so all flops form a long shift register (a scan chain). When SE = 0 it takes the normal functional input D.

  1. Shift in (SE = 1): clock the pattern into all chains, which sets every flop to a chosen state.
  2. Capture (SE = 0): one or two functional clock pulses. The combinational logic responds and the flops capture its outputs.
  3. Shift out (SE = 1): unload the captured response while loading the next pattern; the tester compares it with the expected values.

ATPG (automatic test pattern generation) computes the patterns from the netlist and the fault models, and reports fault coverage. With millions of flops, uncompressed chains would be millions of cycles long, so every modern SoC uses test compression. A few tester channels feed an on-chip decompressor that drives hundreds of short internal chains, and a compactor folds their outputs back down.

Chain length L = N_flops / (N_channels × C) C = compression ratio Test cycles ≈ P × (L + n_capture) + L P = number of patterns Scan time = cycles / f_shift f_shift ≈ 10–100 MHz (power-limited) Data volume ≈ P × L × N_channels × 2 (stimulus + expected response)

Scan design rules

  • All flops scannable. Exceptions (e.g. asynchronous synchronisers) are documented and handled.
  • Asynchronous set/reset controllable from a test pin during scan.
  • No combinational loops; latches transparent or scannable.
  • Clock gating cells get a test-enable override.
  • Chains balanced in length and kept within one clock domain, or joined with lockup latches where they cross domains.
  • Memories and analog wrapped or bypassed so they produce no X values.

Pattern set order

  • Chain test first (shift a 0011… flush pattern): proves the chains shift before trusting any result.
  • Stuck-at patterns: the bulk of coverage, run at slow shift.
  • Transition / at-speed patterns: usually the largest pattern count.
  • Cell-aware and bridging top-up patterns.
  • IDDQ vectors: a handful of static states for current measurement.

Muxes in the digital test logic

Most digital DFT is built from 2:1 muxes controlled by test mode: the scan flop is one, and so are the clock, reset and bypass controls around it. Each gives the tester control or observation of something it could not reach in functional mode. The analog and RF test muxes that route sub-blocks to the on-chip instruments are on the BIST page.

At-speed testing

Digital

Stuck-at testing uses slow clocks, so a defect that only adds delay passes. At-speed tests launch a transition and capture the result one functional clock period later, using the on-chip PLL through an on-chip clock controller (OCC). The tester alone can't deliver two edges hundreds of MHz apart.

SchemeHow the transition is launchedProsCons
Launch-on-capture (LOC)First functional clock after shiftSE can be slow (no at-speed SE routing); functional-like statesLower coverage than LOS; more patterns
Launch-on-shift (LOS)Last shift edge, then SE switches at speedHigher coverage, fewer patternsSE must be timed like a clock; may test non-functional paths (over-kill)
  • Timing-aware ATPG steers transitions through the longest paths (from STA) to catch small delay defects.
  • Multiple clock domains (CPU, DSP/HWA, ADC interface, SPI): test each domain's intra-domain paths separately; inter-domain paths need controlled clock sequences or are masked.
  • Over-test risk: at-speed patterns switch more logic at once than any real workload, so IR drop slows the circuit and good parts can fail. Use power-aware ATPG and low-power fill.

IDDQ testing: measuring quiescent current

Digital

In static CMOS, once the inputs stop switching there is no DC path from VDD to VSS: in every gate either the pull-up or the pull-down network is off. The supply current in that quiet state, I_DDQ (I_DD quiescent), is only leakage. Many defects create a real DC path in some logic states, so the current jumps by µA to mA. IDDQ testing loads a state with scan, stops the clocks, and measures that current. It finds defects that voltage-based (logic) tests often miss, because the circuit may still produce correct logic levels.

Defects IDDQ is good at

DefectWhy current flowsDo logic tests see it?
Bridge between nodes at opposite valuesVDD → on PMOS → bridge → on NMOS → VSSOften not, if one driver is much stronger or the bridge is resistive
Gate-oxide short (pinhole)Gate-to-channel conductionUsually not: the logic still works, but it is a reliability risk (early-life failure)
Open gate / floating inputThe input floats near mid-rail, so both P and N are partly onSometimes; timing-dependent
Stuck-on transistor, punch-throughPull-up and pull-down both conductSometimes
Junction leakage, ESD damageLeaky diode to the substrateNo

How a measurement is made

  1. Load a state with scan, then stop all clocks.
  2. Switch off everything that draws DC current by design: analog bias, bandgap loads, PLL/VCO, ADC, PA/LNA bias, IO pull-ups/downs, IO drivers in a static state, memories in their lowest-leakage mode. No input may float.
  3. Wait for settling. The decoupling capacitance and supply network must reach DC: t ≈ C·ΔV/I. With µF of decap this is 1–10 ms unless the tester uses a fast range and then switches to a precision meter.
  4. Measure I_DD on the core rail with the tester PMU (nA–mA ranges).
  5. Repeat for N vectors (typically 5–50), chosen by ATPG so that every node is set to both 0 and 1 (toggle / pseudo-stuck-at coverage) and likely bridge pairs are set opposite.
Test time ≈ N_vectors × (scan load + t_settle + t_measure) e.g. 20 × (0.5 + 2 + 1) ms ≈ 70 ms → why IDDQ uses few vectors

The advanced-node problem: leakage hides the defect

At 180 nm a good die drew nA to µA, and a defect added µA to mA, so one fixed limit worked. At 40/28/22 nm, subthreshold and gate leakage from millions of gates give a mA-level baseline that varies several-fold with process corner and rises steeply with temperature (roughly 2× per 10–20 °C). A defect adding tens of µA disappears inside that spread. The fix is to compare each die with itself: across vectors, the leakage baseline stays almost constant and only the defect current switches on and off.

In a radar SoC

  • Bypass the on-chip LDOs and supply the digital core rail directly from the tester, so the PMU measures only core current (see the power section).
  • Analog and RF domains off during digital IDDQ. They get their own "static IDD" test with bias on, compared against a characterised range.
  • SRAM leakage (several MB) is a large part of the baseline, so put memories in retention or lowest-leakage mode, or measure the memory rail separately.
  • Usually at wafer probe, at room or cold temperature, right after the scan chain test. It pairs well with HVST: IDDQ before and after the stress.

Radar-SoC specifics: scan in a mixed-signal chip

DigitalSupply

Keep the RF and analog safe

  • During scan, register bits that control analog blocks change at random. Force a safe state through test-mode gating: PA and LO off, VCO disabled, LDOs in a known mode, eFuse programming locked.
  • Put the analog control registers in wrapper cells that hold their value during shift and are tested by themselves.
  • An accidental PA enable during scan can exceed EIRP limits on the tester or stress the device.

Shift power vs on-chip LDOs

  • Random fill makes about 50% of flops toggle every shift cycle. That can be several times the functional current, with a large di/dt.
  • On-chip LDOs (see the power section) are sized for functional load. Shift current can drop the rail below the retention or timing margin, and good dies fail.
  • Mitigations: low-power (fill-adjacent) patterns, lower shift frequency, staggered chain clocks, and supplying the core rail from the ATE during scan.

Analog-digital boundaries

  • ADC outputs are X sources during scan. Mask them, or insert observe/control test points at the digital boundary.
  • Test the DFE and interfaces with a digital loopback (pattern generator → DFE → capture memory) as well as scan.
  • Test the ADC, DAC and RF analog content with the BIST/BISM loopbacks on the BIST page.

Clocks for at-speed

  • The OCC takes the digital PLL clock (not the 20 GHz RF synthesiser) and gates exactly the launch and capture pulses.
  • The reference crystal comes from the ATE during test, so at-speed frequencies are exact.
  • Lockstep CPUs: test both cores, and include the comparator logic (a latent-fault target).

Memory BIST and repair

Digital

SRAMs are dense and defect-prone, and they aren't reached efficiently through scan. An on-chip MBIST controller for each memory group generates addresses and data, compares read data, and logs failures. It runs at speed on all memories in parallel (within power limits).

Logic BIST and in-field (key-on) test

DigitalISO 26262

Logic BIST reuses the scan chains but replaces the tester with on-chip hardware: an LFSR/PRPG generates pseudo-random patterns, and a MISR compresses all responses into one signature. At the end, the signature is compared with the golden value stored on-chip. It needs no tester, which is why it's the standard way to test logic in the car.

AspectTypical choice
When it runsKey-on (before radar output is trusted); some SoCs also periodically, per core, while the other lockstep partner covers
Time budget≈ 10–50 ms at start-up, within the system's boot budget
What it coversLatent faults in safety mechanisms: lockstep comparators, ECC logic, watchdogs, monitors (ISO 26262 LFM)
Runtime (single-point) faultsCovered instead by lockstep, ECC, CRC, watchdog, plus analog/RF monitors
HybridThe same decompressor/LFSR hardware serves both ATE compression and LBIST, which saves area

Stress conditions and screening

Screening

Some defects are latent. The part passes a normal test today but fails after weeks in the car: thin gate-oxide spots, marginal vias, weak SRAM cells. Screening applies stress (voltage, temperature, time) to make those defects fail at test, and statistics to reject parts that pass but look abnormal.

Statistical screens (outlier rejection)

PAT / DPAT (AEC-Q001)

Part Average Testing: set limits from the population's robust mean ± 6σ, not just the spec. Dynamic PAT recalculates them per lot or wafer. Parts inside spec but outside the population are likely defective and get rejected.

Robust μ = median, σ = IQR / 1.35 PAT limits = μ ± 6σ (inside spec limits)

Geographic screens

GDBN (good die in bad neighbourhood) and NNR (nearest-neighbour residual) use the wafer map. A passing die surrounded by failing dies, or whose parameters differ from its neighbours', sits in a defect cluster and is rejected. Z-PAT looks at the same die position across wafers. What the patterns mean in the fab: wafer map signatures.

Part-level correlation

Multivariate screens combine parameters such as IDDQ vs Vmin vs ring-oscillator speed. A part whose combination doesn't fit its process corner is an outlier even when each value is normal. The trim values from BIST/BISM (see the BIST page) are useful inputs.

Reliability qualification (AEC-Q100)

Qualification

Qualification is done once per product (and after major changes) on sample lots. It proves the design and process meet the automotive lifetime. Screening then keeps every shipped part in line with the qualified population. The grade sets the ambient range: Grade 1 is −40…+125 °C, Grade 0 is −40…+150 °C, and radar is typically Grade 1 or 2.

Production test flow (digital + stress)

  1. Wafer probe (CP), insertion 1 at 25 °C: Cres + open/short + power-short → leakage → scan chain test + stuck-at → IDDQ → HVST → IDDQ again → MBIST + repair analysis → VLV scan/MBIST → reference trims and analog/RF BIST/BISM (see the BIST page) → eFuse burn (repair + trims) → verify. Insertion 2 at 125 °C: at-speed transition scan with guard band, hot leakage. The complete flow is on the production test flow page.
  2. Post-probe data: DPAT limits, GDBN/NNR on the wafer map, ink out the outliers.
  3. Assembly (FC-BGA / eWLB).
  4. Final test (FT), cold and hot: reduced scan (at-speed with guard band), MBIST, LBIST self-check, BIST/BISM re-check at temperature, PAT on the key parameters.
  5. Burn-in (if required for the product or customer) → post-burn-in test.
  6. Optional system-level test (SLT): boot the radar firmware and run a chirp frame with loopback; catches interaction defects that structural test misses.
  7. Data feed-forward: per-part data (ECID) links wafer, final and field returns for failure analysis.

DFT checklist for a radar SoC

Architecture

  • Coverage targets agreed with the customer per fault model (stuck-at, transition, cell-aware, memory)
  • Compression ratio sized to tester channels and memory
  • OCC on every at-speed clock domain; PLL usable in test mode
  • MBIST on all memories, with repair and eFuse; ECC testable
  • LBIST for key-on latent-fault coverage, with a time budget and test points
  • IJTAG network reaching MBIST, LBIST, sensors, trims and analog BIST
  • Test/debug access locked in production

Mixed-signal and stress

  • Safe state for PA, LO, VCO and LDOs whenever scan is active
  • Shift power budget checked against on-chip LDO capability; option to supply the rail from the ATE
  • X sources (ADC, memories, analog) masked or bounded
  • HVST mode: core rail can be forced high safely; IO and analog protected
  • VLV and retention modes supported (regulator bypass)
  • ECID and per-part datalog for PAT, GDBN and field-return traceability
Values are typical industry practice for automotive SoCs and are meant for planning. Actual coverage targets, stress levels and qualification conditions come from your customer requirements, the foundry, and the current AEC-Q100 / AEC-Q001 / ISO 26262 documents.
