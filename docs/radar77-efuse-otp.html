eFuse, OTP and trim storage

TrimTest

Process spread moves every analog block: a bandgap can be off by ±2–3 %, an RC filter corner by ±20 %, a power detector by a dB or more. Trimming measures each part and stores a correction code that stays with the chip for life. The storage is a small one-time-programmable memory, usually eFuses or antifuse OTP, written once at wafer sort or final test and read into registers every time the chip starts. Where the codes come from is on the BIST page; this page is about how they are stored, programmed and kept correct.

eFuse bit cell, sensing and the shadow register

Programming energy E ≈ V_PP · I_PGM · t_PGM e.g. 2.0 V × 20 mA × 5 µs = 0.2 µJ per bit Resistance ratio R_blown / R_intact > 50–100 (read decision at R_ref ≈ 2–5 kΩ) Margin read normal: R_ref ; "blown" check: R_ref × 0.5 ; "intact" check: R_ref × 2 Program time serial burn of N bits: N × (t_PGM + overhead) e.g. 1 024 × 10 µs ≈ 10 ms
  • Program supply: VPP comes from a dedicated tester pin or an on-chip charge pump enabled only in test mode. Its drop under 20 mA decides whether a fuse blows fully, so it is force/sense controlled.
  • One bit at a time: fuses are burned serially so the supply only sees one programming current; an array of 1–4 kbit takes milliseconds.
  • Antifuse cells use a high voltage (5–7 V) on a thin oxide through a thick-oxide select device; no large current, so arrays can be larger and denser.
  • Placement: fuse arrays sit away from RF blocks and are shielded; the programming current and VPP routing must not couple into the LO or the antennas.

The trim flow

Order matters. References first: bandgap → aux ADC → temperature sensor → detectors → LDOs and bias → filters and RF. A wrong bandgap trim shifts every later measurement in the same direction (BIST: feeding results back).

A typical radar SoC uses 1–4 kbit of OTP. The trim codes themselves are also a valuable process monitor: their distribution per lot is charted like any WAT parameter, and a shift shows a process change before yield moves.

Reliability and safety of stored trim

  • Retention: trim must survive 15 years at up to 150 °C. NVM retention and endurance are part of qualification (AEC-Q100-005; reliability page).
  • Integrity at boot: the boot sequence reads all words, checks CRC and ECC, and refuses to start the RF (or reports a fault) if they are wrong. This is a key-on safety mechanism (key-on tests).
  • Locking: after final test a lock bit disables programming and test-mode access, so nothing in the field can rewrite the trim (cybersecurity).
  • Field updates: calibration that changes in the field (temperature tables, antenna calibration from module EOL) goes to the module's flash, not to on-chip OTP.

Test-flow and cost view

  • Most trims are burned at wafer sort, where test is cheapest and GDBN/PAT can still use the data; RF trims that need the package (detector slope, PA bias) are burned at final test (test insertions).
  • Burning adds a few ms; the margin read and re-measure add more, but skipping them is how latent trim escapes happen.
  • Trim codes and the die ID are logged with the datalog, so a field return can be traced to its wafer, its PCM data and its trim history (8D / RMA).
  • A worked example of why trim matters for Tx power is on the worked-example page.
